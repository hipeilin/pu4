\abstract{
Understanding user interactions in digital systems is essential in analyzing user behaviors and improving system usability. However, a collection of interaction sequences is often large and unstructured, making it challenging to uncover interaction patterns. 
To address this challenge, we introduce a visual analytics approach that integrates hierarchical clustering and process mining techniques to support analysts in exploring unstructured, large interaction sequence data. 
Our system employs a tailored dynamic time warping-based similarity measure to enable comparison of interaction sequences.
Based on the sequence similarities, we provide stepwise, interactive navigation of clustering results with contextual visual cues for refinement and validation.
We further apply process mining to characterize derived clusters. 
Through these hierarchical clustering and process mining steps, analysts can progressively uncover meaningful interaction patterns while utilizing visual guidance and incorporating domain expertise.
We demonstrate our system's effectiveness and applicability through two case studies involving system designers, developers, and domain experts.
}

\subsection{Event Sequence Similarity Measures and Visualization}

Various event sequence similarity measures have been developed to facilitate sequence comparison, each offering distinct advantages and limitations~\cite{Sabrina08Distance, Studer15What}.
These developed measures can be classified based on data type and the underlying methodological framework, such as lock-step and elastic measures~\cite{Ding08Querying, Saeed15Time, Saeed06Novel, Zielezinski17Alignment}.
Lock-step measures, such as the Euclidean distance~\cite{Faloutsos94Fast}, perform a direct point-wise comparison of values between two sequences.
These measures are sensitive to temporal drifts, misalignments, noise, or ``distortion'' in the sequences~\cite{Ratanamahatana05Novel}.
Elastic measures overcome these issues by allowing sequences to be ``stretched'' or ``compressed'' to detect similar patterns.
% To address these limitations, elastic measures introduce temporal flexibility by allowing a sequence to be ``stretched'' or ``compressed'' when identifying similar patterns. 
One prominent example is DTW~\cite{Berndt94Using}, which aligns sequences that vary in speed or have temporal shifts, improving the robustness in assessing similarity.
% Edit distance-based measures, including longest common subsequence~\cite{Andre97Using}, also fall into this category.

% However, the elastic measures often with high computational costs and require parameter tuning, such as the warping window size~\cite{Ding08Querying}. 
% For example, a single DTW calculation has a time complexity of $O(nw)$, where \textit{n} represents the length of the sequence, and \textit{w} is the warping window size~\cite{Ding08Querying}.
% Finally, pattern-based measures, such as SpADe~\cite{Chen07SpADe}, identify matching segments within the entire sequence, referred to as ``patterns'', by permitting shifts and scaling in both the temporal and amplitude dimensions.
% One limitation is the reliance on predefining and tuning of parameters, including the minimum support, temporal/amplitude scale factor, pattern length, and sliding step size, among others~\cite{Ding08Querying}.
% Pattern-based measures, such as SpADe~\cite{Chen07SpADe}, identify matching segments within the entire sequence, referred to as ``patterns''.
% It focuses on discovering recurring, possibly misaligned patterns by permitting shifts and scaling in both the temporal and amplitude dimensions.
% Other pattern mining approaches, such as Apriori-based sequence mining~\cite{Agrawal95Mining} and PrefixSpan~\cite{Jian01PrefixSpan}, also aim to extract frequent subsequences from large datasets.
% While Apriori-based methods generate candidate patterns iteratively, PrefixSpan reduces computational overhead by utilizing a pattern growth approach to mine frequent sequential patterns directly.
% These techniques often rely on user-defined parameters, such as minimum support, which can impact the quality and quantity of discovered patterns.
% Another limitation is their sensitivity to parameter tuning, including temporal and amplitude scaling factors, pattern length, and sliding step size, which require careful selection to balance pattern coverage and computational efficiency~\cite{Ding08Querying}.

In this work, we primarily examine discrete event sequences where events occur in irregular periods and event attributes may be categorical or numerical. 
% Unlike univariate time series, these sequences do not adhere to a consistent or uniformly spaced temporal structure~\cite{Wang2004Scalable}.
% Generally, there are three distinct objectives when investigating event sequence similarity: 
% Investing event sequence similarity has three main objectives: identifying similarity in (1) time, (2) shape, and (3) change (structural similarity)~\cite{Bagnall05Clustering}.
% Our focus is on the overall shape of the sequence, and the exact timing of patterns is not crucial. 
Due to this irregularity, elastic methods such as DTW are suitable to calculate the similarity of discrete event sequences~\cite{Keogh05Clustering, Saeed15Time}.
% It has been widely adopted in domains, such as data mining and time series clustering~\cite{Bernard25IVESA, Rongchen20Comparative, Jin20CarePre, Guo19Visual}.
% Its main strength is the robust capability to cope with time deformations and varying speeds inherent in time-dependent data~\cite{Bagnall27The, Muller07Dynamic, Senin09Dynamic}.
% We investigate both dependent (i.e., multi-dimensional) and independent DTW approaches~\cite{Shokoohi17Generalizing}.
% Motivated by the nature of our data, capturing multiple event types over time (i.e., multi-dimensional), we investigate both dependent and independent DTW approaches~\cite{Shokoohi17Generalizing}.
User interaction logs usually consist of multiple event types (e.g., different interaction commands), forming multi-dimensional time series with irregular periods.
To handle multi-dimensional time series, we can apply dependent or independent DTW methods~\cite{Shokoohi17Generalizing}.
The dependent DTW method generates a unified warping path across all dimensions simultaneously, ensuring that temporal alignment is maintained across multiple attributes. 
In contrast, the independent DTW method applies standard DTW to each dimension separately and aggregates the individual distance calculations to derive a final similarity measure.
Our tailored similarity measure employs the independent DTW method as user interactions rarely involve multiple events simultaneously (\cref{sec:seq_similaity}).

\subsection{Event Sequence Summarization}

Event sequence summarization plays a critical role in grouping sequences to assist the discovery of common behaviors, anomalies, and major progression patterns. 
Clustering is commonly used to summarize sequences~\cite{Guo22Survey}, with methods like $k$-means~\cite{Forgy65Cluster,Vakali05Overview} and hierarchical clustering~\cite{Berkhin06Survey,Ali19Clustering}.
%Clustering methods are commonly employed to generate summaries of sequences~\cite{Guo22Survey}. Representative methods include $k$-means~\cite{Forgy65Cluster,Vakali05Overview} and hierarchical clustering~\cite{Berkhin06Survey,Ali19Clustering}. 
% Clustering methodologies can be broadly categorized into partitional, such as $k$-means~\cite{Forgy65Cluster}, expectation maximization~\cite{Redner84Mixture}, and hierarchical approaches~\cite{Berkhin06Survey}, with the latter further subdivided into divisive (top-down) and agglomerative (bottom-up) techniques~\cite{Everitt11Cluster, Ali19Clustering}.
% Other notable clustering strategies include graph-based, density-based, hybrid (combined), incremental, and kernel-based hierarchical clustering methods.
% Partitional algorithms often exhibit nonlinear computational complexity during runtime, despite efforts for improvement~\cite{Vakali05Overview}.
% Additionally, in many cases, the number of clusters (\( k \)) must be predetermined~\cite{Ali19Clustering}.
When focusing on interactive clustering of event sequences, hierarchical clustering provides a strong benefit as it produces a dendrogram: a tree structure that depicts the hierarchical relationships between data points and clusters~\cite{Ran23Comprehensive}.
% However, once a merging or division is done at one level of the hierarchy, it cannot be undone later~\cite{Ali19Clustering}, meaning that there is no mechanism to incorporate domain knowledge for refining or merging clusters.
% In this work, hierarchical clustering is well-suited, as it does not require a predefined number of clusters.
% Not only does it facilitate the identification of outliers and unusual groupings, but it also produces a dendrogram to visualize relationships.
Thus, the dendrogram provides information regarding clustering quality and cluster sizes at each hierarchy level, enabling the identification of outliers and undesired grouping. 
Our VA workflow builds upon a hierarchical clustering approach but extends it to support flexible navigation and informed dendrogram partitioning for cluster extraction. 
Additionally, we introduce the capability to merge similar clusters that are not adjacent in the dendrogram, enhancing customizability and adaptability in the clustering process.

\subsection{Process Mining in Visual Analytics}

VA and process mining have evolved independently, with little mutual awareness and cross-disciplinary integration~\cite{Miksch24Visual, Gschwandtner17Visual}, and traditionally prioritized mining methods~\cite{Rinderle23Process, Daniel12Business}.
As a result, each field has developed distinct foundational concepts and terminology, limiting their convergence and collaborative advancements.
Additionally, there is limited support for human-centric decision-making and analytical reasoning through interactive visualizations~\cite{Di24Human, Keim08Visual}.
Consequently, there has been a growing emphasis on the need for deeper integration between visual analytics and process mining~\cite{Kriglstein16Visual, Gschwandtner17Visual, Miksch24Visual} and the development of novel visualization and interaction techniques~\cite{Klinkmuller19Mining, García18Complete, Jana22Process}.

Until recently, limited research had explored integrated approaches leveraging concepts from both process mining and visual analytics in a cohesive manner.
Among these works, the use of directed graphs to represent the process model is commonly utilized~\cite{Maguire13Visual, Hipp12Navigating, Christian07Fuzzy}.
While these works applied graph layout algorithms to visualize directed graphs, our work employs an arc diagram design instead. 
This design mitigates the complexity and visual clutter in process model visualization~\cite{Miksch24Visual}.
%, which often fails to provide a thorough representation of the workflow~\cite{Miksch24Visual}.

Establishing explicit connections between mined process representations and their original data sources remains a significant open challenge~\cite{Zerbato23Supporting}.
A few visualizations provide summarized representations of individual event sequences to identify common sequence paths~\cite{Bodesinsky15Exploration, Song07Supporting}.
To identify deviations and discrepancies from existing process models, researchers computed and visualized the conformance of individual event sequences~\cite{Adriansyah11Conformance, Bodesinsky13Visual}.
We introduce a novel workflow that integrates conformance analysis and establishes a connection between individual event sequences and the process model, facilitating analysts to identify recurring and discrepant patterns in individual sequences.

% 2. Computation of the similarity of the interaction sequences
\subsection{Sequence Similarity}
\label{sec:seq_similaity}

Following preprocessing, we use DTW to compute event sequence similarities.
However, DTW is designed to operate on \textit{continuous} time series data.
Thus, our similarity computation first generates a continuous-time series-like structure for each event type. 
To do so, we insert intermediate events by duplicating the most recent event at one-second intervals until the subsequent event occurs.
This temporal resolution is consistent with our preprocessing strategy, which aggregates the data at a one-second resolution, thereby avoiding excessive granularity at the millisecond scale.
Given that the interaction log comprises four primary event types, as described in~\cref{preprocessing}, this process results in four distinct univariate time series per user, each corresponding to one of the event types.
% In our case, for each user, this process results in four separate univariate time series corresponding to the four event types' sequences.

% We considered both the dependent and independent variants of DTW~\cite{Shokoohi17Generalizing}. 
To compare each pair of users' multiple univariate time series with DTW, we can employ the dependent or independent variant of DTW~\cite{Shokoohi17Generalizing}.
The dependent DTW ($ DTW_{D} $) assumes that all variables are mutually dependent, enforcing the same warping path across all variables.
% While this approach ensures temporal consistency among all univariate time series,
Due to this enforcement, $ DTW_{D} $ is less effective in scenarios where temporal lag or asynchronous behavior exists across event types~\cite{Shokoohi17Generalizing}.
In contrast, the independent DTW ($ DTW_{I} $) computes a separate alignment path for each univariate time series, allowing separate warping paths for each variable.
This is more appropriate when variables are lagged or loosely coupled.
The SiN log dataset falls into such conditions since users cannot simultaneously engage with the interface and change attribute filters.
% Due to the above consideration, we employ $ DTW_{I} $.
Therefore, $ DTW_{I} $ is utilized.
% Therefore, we can consider interactions with the exhibit interface and attribute modifications that occur asynchronously, and the resulting event sequences represent distinct temporal dimensions.

Finally, both $DTW_{D}$ and $DTW_{I}$ fundamentally rely on the same underlying similarity computation.
The dependent variant ($DTW_{D}$) operates analogously to traditional DTW for univariate time series, utilizing the cumulative squared Euclidean distance across the shared alignment path.
Conversely, the independent variant ($DTW_{I}$) computes DTW alignments separately for each dimension and aggregates the resulting distances, each based on Euclidean distance~\cite{Shokoohi17Generalizing}.
However, in symbolic sequences (e.g., categorical values), such distance metrics become semantically inappropriate, as the magnitude of numerical encodings can influence the similarity assessment between the discrete events. 
% To address this, we implement a custom distance function tailored to categorical data, which returns a binary output: 0 for identical events and 1 for any mismatches.
To address this, we implement a custom distance function that returns $0$ for matches and $1$ for mismatches, as follows:
% \[
% customDist(e_1, e_2) =
% \begin{cases}
% 0, & \text{if } e_1 = e_2 \\
% 1, & \text{if } e_1 \ne e_2
% \end{cases}
% \]
\begin{equation}
customDist(e_1, e_2) =
\begin{cases}
0, & \text{if } e_1 = e_2 \\
1, & \text{if } e_1 \ne e_2
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

After computing DTW distances, pairwise similarities are obtained across all sequences, resulting in a symmetric $n \times n$ distance matrix, where $n$ is the number of event sequences/users. 
This matrix serves as the input for subsequent hierarchical clustering.

We use hierarchical clustering to avoid predefining the number of clusters and enable flexible exploration across multiple levels of granularity.

It also produces a dendrogram showing inter-cluster relationships (\textbf{T5}), which is commonly applied to time-series data~\cite{Ali19Clustering}.

\subsection{Cluster Description}

After clustering, it is essential to generate a concise and interpretable summary of each cluster's interaction behaviors (\textbf{T3}).
To this end, we leverage process mining techniques, specifically process maps~\cite{van16Data}.
% to characterize the temporal structure of activities within each cluster.
A process map typically consists of nodes (activities) and directed arcs that capture precedence relations, indicating that one activity directly follows another.
We adopt the directly follows graph (DFG) model as the basis for our process representation.
% commonly used in process mining, as the basis for our process representation.

We use the Python library PM4Py~\cite{Alessandro19Process} and its built-in functionality to derive a DFG from each cluster’s event log.
Note that, unlike the sequence similarity computation, we utilize the preprocessed event log data without applying temporal interpolation, preserving the original event states.
The resulting process graph is then post-processed to extract nodes and links from the PM4Py output.
% which are serialized into a standardized JSON format. 
We impose a fixed ordering of nodes within the process graphs to enhance visual comparison and identification of similarities and differences between process models (\textbf{T6}).
This output is subsequently used for front-end rendering and interactive visualization, as described in~\cref{subsubsec:process_description_pane}.

% 5. Computation of conformance to respective models 
\subsection{Model Conformance}

Conformance checking serves as a natural extension within the process mining paradigm, offering a principled method to assess the consistency between observed behaviors and the discovered models.
To evaluate how well individual event sequences align with a representative process model, we perform conformance checking.
This analysis supports the identification of potential outliers and the integration of domain expertise into the cluster interpretation process (\textbf{T7}).

Given that we utilize the output of DFGs as our process model representation, we adopt a conformance measure specifically designed to compare individual traces against DFGs.
To this end, we leverage the alignment-based~\cite{Adriansyah11Conformance} conformance checking submodule of the PM4Py library~\cite{Alessandro19Process}, which applies the alignment algorithm tailored to DFGs to compare individual log/trace objects and a process model.
Each sequence is assigned a fitness score, quantifying the degree of alignment with the model.
This score can be interpreted as the degree of behavioral consistency between each sequence and the expected direct-follow relations in the model.
% In our implementation, we iterate over individual cases in the cluster, extract their corresponding event logs, and compute the fitness scores.
These scores enable the ranking of sequences (\textbf{T4}), allowing analysts to quickly detect the most and least conforming sequences within a cluster, as described in further detail in~\cref{subsec:sequence_view}.

\subsubsection{Cluster Pane}
\label{subsubsec:cluster_pane}

The left pane (\cref{fig:teaser}.1) presents a high-level summary of the top levels of the hierarchical clustering structure (\textbf{T1}). 
Previous work has explored aggregated dendrogram~\cite{Liu20Aggregated} and dendrogram-matrix representations~\cite{Chen09Constructing}.
Inspired by these, we introduce a summarizing \textit{cluster matrix} representation encoded using a combination of a partial dendrogram with abstract isosceles triangular glyphs and a lower-triangular similarity matrix (see~\cref{fig:cluster_mapping}), enabling users to comprehend intra- and inter-cluster relationships and structural conciseness at a glance (\textbf{T5}).

In a traditional dendrogram, the branches reflect the original hierarchical relationships among individual data points or clusters, while the vertical height of each merge encodes the dissimilarity between two child clusters.
We map the dendrogram hierarchy in the proposed cluster matrix representation in which the cluster nodes represent subclusters that are interactively expanded to reveal the next hierarchy level (\textbf{T1}). 
Intra-/inter- similarity is mapped on the color of each cluster matrix node, forming a lower-triangular similarity matrix that conveys pairwise cluster relationships. 
This design choice preserves screen space (\textbf{T1}) while maintaining the consistency of the matrix (\textbf{T5}).
We utilize a partial dendrogram to convey structural information from the hierarchical clustering, preserving the connectivity and their associated heights, thus reducing visual clutter (\textbf{T1}).

%Additionally, the merge height serves as a visual indicator to support user-driven refinement of the clustering granularity, providing cues as to which clusters are to be merged or subdivided when the number of clusters is manually decreased or increased, respectively.

An isosceles triangle glyph is rendered adjacent to each cluster node. The base width of the triangle glyph encodes the number of sequences within the cluster, and the height corresponds to the distance between the clusters at the next step of the hierarchy, as illustrated in~\cref{fig:cluster_mapping}, providing a visual cue for assessing cluster similarity.
% merge height in the dendrogram.
This design conveys both the cluster size and its relative position within the hierarchical structure (\textbf{T5}). It serves as a visual indicator to support user-driven refinement of clustering granularity, providing cues as to which clusters to merge or subdivide when the number of clusters is manually decreased or increased, respectively.
Detailed information about cluster composition, such as its size and the identifiers of the sequences composing it, can be accessed on hover (\textbf{T2}). 
The triangle glyphs are also interactive and serve as entry points for expanding individual clusters (cf.~\cref{subsec:interaction}). 
The number of clusters is determined by counting the subclusters that remain unexpanded.

The initial number of clusters is determined using a method analogous to the elbow criterion (\textbf{T3}).
% the system employs a largest gap heuristic algorithm to automatically estimate and recommend the optimal number of clusters by detecting the most prominent separation within the data. 
The point of the largest increase in linkage distance (i.e., the most significant ``gap'') is identified to suggest a suitable cut in the hierarchy structure. 
Nevertheless, the system provides interactive flexibility, allowing users to manually adjust the number of clusters and explore the impact of increasing or decreasing this number.
However, the triangle glyph height does not convey the internal conciseness of individual clusters. 
To address this limitation, we compute intra-cluster conciseness scores by averaging all pairwise distances among sequences within each cluster, and inter-cluster dissimilarity scores by averaging distances between sequences across clusters.
Since these two measures capture fundamentally different aspects of cluster structure and are not directly comparable, we provide a toggle mechanism to enable selective display of intra- or inter-cluster scores through color encoding on the matrix (\textbf{T5}).
Finally, to effectively use the remaining space of the matrix, we incorporate simplified/compressed process model thumbnails to provide a glimpse of the behavioral patterns within each cluster (\textbf{T3}).

\subsubsection{Process Description Pane}
\label{subsubsec:process_description_pane}

The middle pane (\cref{fig:teaser}.2) presents cluster descriptions derived by process mining (\textbf{T3}) and facilitates cluster description comparison (\textbf{T6}). 
These descriptions are visualized as arc diagrams and follow the same visual encoding as the process thumbnails used in the \textit{Cluster Pane}.

A fixed number and fixed ordering of the nodes is enforced to enhance visual comparability across clusters (\textbf{T6}).
Each process model consistently begins with a ``Start'' node, represented by a triangle symbolizing a play icon, and terminates at an ``End'' node, depicted as a square resembling a stop icon.
All intermediate nodes are color-coded distinctly according to the four defined event types.
Directed arcs between node pairs represent transitions within the process.
The flow direction is encoded via spatial positioning; arcs positioned above the node axis indicate forward transitions from left to right, while arcs below signify transitions in the reverse direction.
Arc thickness encodes the frequency of transitions between the nodes. 
To capture and visually integrate self-loop behaviors for each node, we utilize the border thickness, where thicker borders denote a higher number of self-transitions.
Arcs within the process model visualization can be filtered through the controls provided in the \textit{Cluster and Process Control Panel}, enabling users to focus on prominent transitions and reduce visual complexity.

To the left of each process model, the corresponding cluster ID is displayed, while the number of sequences in the cluster is shown to the right. 
This layout reinforces the association between each process description and its respective cluster (\textbf{T9}).
Additionally, small squares rendered beneath each arc diagram provide a supplementary visual cue of cluster size. 
If a characterization of log sessions exists, it can be mapped to the color of these.
These enhance visual discernibility and comparison across models.
On the far right of each process model, a selection checkbox allows users to initiate model merging operations and link the cluster to detailed sequence-level visualizations (\textbf{T9}).

\subsubsection{Process Merging Pane}

The rightmost pane (\cref{fig:teaser}.3) will dynamically display the process models that have been manually selected for merging based on expert-driven assessment (\textbf{T8}).
These models are rendered using the same arc diagram encoding introduced in the \textit{Process Description Pane}, ensuring visual consistency across views (\textbf{T9}).
The models shown in this pane represent candidate clusters that, upon detailed examination and comparison, were identified by users as exhibiting sufficiently similar exploration patterns or strategies. 
These merged models can subsequently be exported, ready for downstream analysis or further synthesis.
Further details regarding interactions and coordinated linking between panes and views are described in \cref{subsec:interaction}.

The latter component is configured and controlled through the former.

To mitigate this limitation, we employ a consistent color-coding scheme across the sequence and process model visualizations, enabling users to efficiently associate individual actions with their corresponding modeled behaviors.

\subsection{Interaction with reVISID}
\label{subsec:interaction}

The interactions facilitated by the system correspond to stages 2 through 5 of the analytical pipeline~\cref{fig:workflow}.
While the workflow generally follows a sequential progression, it is inherently iterative. 
Users may repeatedly adjust the number of clusters in stage 2 (e.g., by increasing or decreasing the dendrogram cut), proceed to stage 3 to compare the resulting process models, and examine individual sequences in stage 4.
If the outcomes at any stage are deemed unsatisfactory, users can iterate and refine previous steps, enabling a flexible and exploratory analysis process.
Finally, users arrive at a set of identified exploration strategies by selectively merging and exporting process models.

The \textbf{Cluster Pane} within the \textsc{Cluster and Process View} supports stepwise exploration of the hierarchical clustering structure. 
Users can inspect individual clusters in detail, accessing information such as cluster size, constituent sequence identifiers, and intra-cluster conciseness (\textbf{T2}).
Manual control over the number of clusters is provided by moving the cut level of the dendrogram upward or downward, effectively adjusting the granularity of the clustering (\textbf{T9}).
The height of each triangle glyph serves as a visual indicator of the distance between two subclusters, offering cues as to which cluster is to be split or merged when adjusting the cluster count.
When multiple clusters are visible, users can assess inter-cluster similarity and refer to the associated simplified process model thumbnails to evaluate whether structurally similar clusters can potentially be grouped (\textbf{T5}, \textbf{T8}).
The detailed process model corresponding to each cluster is displayed in the \textit{Process Description Pane}.
Users can examine the process model in detail and compare whether some of the models are similar (\textbf{T6}).
If a particular subcluster contains a large number of sequences, as indicated by a wide triangle glyph, and exhibits low conciseness with limited interpretability in the corresponding process model, it potentially requires further investigation.
By default, the triangle glyph width is scaled locally concerning the current set of subclusters being examined.
However, it could mislead users when performing cross-cluster comparisons due to inconsistent scale references.
To mitigate this issue, users can toggle between local and global scaling modes, enabling more accurate interpretation of cluster sizes across varying hierarchical levels.

Users can expand a cluster by clicking on its associated triangle glyph.
Upon expansion, child subclusters are shown with an estimated optimal number of subdivisions (\textbf{T3}).
The process models in the \textit{Process Description Pane} are updated accordingly.
The linking between a specific cluster in the \textit{Cluster Pane} and its corresponding process model in the \textit{Process Description Pane} can be activated by hovering (\textbf{T9}).

To support detailed analysis of sequences within specific clusters, users can select the corresponding process models in the \textit{Process Description Pane} and update the \textsc{Sequence View} to display the individual event sequences associated with the selected model, enabling users to examine how the process unfolds over time (\textbf{T4}, \textbf{T9}).
To further validate whether certain clusters can be grouped, users may activate the conformance checking functionality by selecting a specific model.
This triggers an automatic evaluation of all sequences against the selected model and sorts them by descending conformance score, as illustrated in~\cref{fig:teaser}.5.
Doing so allows users to assess how well each sequence aligns with the abstracted model (\textbf{T7}) and enables them, based on their domain expertise, to identify outliers or assess whether low-conforming sequences align semantically with the cluster or should undergo reclassification.
% In doing so, based on their domain expertise, users can identify potential outliers or determine whether low-conforming sequences are semantically consistent with the cluster assignment or should undergo reclassification.
This interactive and informed validation process reinforces the coherence of the cluster structure of the derived models.

The layout of the interface is designed to support flexible allocation of screen space across the three main panes (\textbf{T2}).
Users can dynamically resize each pane via dragging interactions to prioritize specific analytical tasks.
Within each pane, independent zooming and panning functionalities are provided, enabling seamless transitions between overview and detail~\cite{Shneiderman1996The} (\textbf{T2}).
This interaction design facilitates scalable exploration.
For example, users can zoom out in the \textit{Cluster Pane} to gain a global perspective of the cluster hierarchy structure, including the positioning and hierarchical context of the currently selected cluster.
Similarly, users can zoom out in the \textit{Process Description Pane} to gain an overview of multiple process models, supporting easier visual comparison and selection.

This exploratory process is iteratively repeated until a sufficiently meaningful set of clusters with distinct and interpretable process models is identified.
The selected process models can be merged and subsequently exported to the \textit{Process Output Pane} for further analysis (\textbf{T8}).
The visual representation of merged models adheres to the same arc diagram convention established in the \textit{Process Description Pane}.
The total number of constituent sequences resulting from the merge, as well as the identifiers of the originating clusters, are displayed.

\subsection{Case Study 1: Exhibit Design Analysis}

Understanding how visitors interact with public exhibits is critical for optimizing system design and enhancing user experience. 
Equally important is adopting an educational perspective to interpret observed behaviors, uncover patterns, and formulate hypotheses about users' exploration and cognitive processes.

We conducted two collaborative workshops to analyze the collected in-the-wild interaction log dataset.
The first workshop involved the original exhibit designer and one of the system developers, hereafter referred to as the ``SiN design experts''.
The second workshop was conducted with two co-authors and experts in visual learning and communication, hereafter referred to as the ``VLC experts''. 
For clarity, we collectively refer to both groups as ``experts''.
During the workshops, we provided an overview of the analysis process enabled by the reVISID system, detailing the specific functionalities and interactions available to support exploratory data investigation. 
Following this, we commenced an exploration session where the authors were navigating reVISID under the instruction of the experts.

On startup, reVISID suggested splitting the dataset into two clusters, with a larger cluster consisting of 112 sequences and a smaller cluster consisting of 5 sequences, providing a starting point for analysis.
The domain experts began their exploration by conducting a preliminary examination of the two initial cluster descriptions, by inspecting the process arc diagrams to gain an overview of the clustered interaction patterns.
The larger cluster predominantly exhibited a pattern where users opened infopanels but did not perform further interactions within the exhibit, indicating minimal engagement.
In contrast, the smaller cluster demonstrated more complex interaction behaviors, characterized by frequent attribute changes and repeated revisits to previously opened infopanels.
Arc filtering was applied to refine the analysis further and differentiate more distinctly the prominent interaction patterns.
%A clearer differentiation between the behavioral patterns across clusters could be observed.

The experts then proceeded to the \textsc{Sequence View} to inspect the detailed interaction sequences composing each cluster, aiming to analyze variations in user interaction patterns with the exhibit.
This investigation focuses on key aspects, including interaction behaviors (e.g., attribute changes), exploration patterns (e.g., the extent of infopanel engagement, frequency of revisits to previously opened infopanels), and interaction duration.
The inspection revealed the smaller cluster was characterized by longer session durations (on average 15 minutes) with multiple user interactions with the exhibit.
On the other hand, the larger cluster primarily comprised shorter sessions with very limited engagement, as indicated also by the cluster description. 
Upon viewing the difference in the interaction sequences of the smaller cluster, the SiN design experts commented on the possible presence of on-site guides who regularly demonstrate exhibits to visiting groups, subsequently encouraging the visitors to engage and interact.
These prolonged sessions, which may appear as outliers, could potentially be indicative of such interactions by guides and visiting groups.

Further partitioning of the larger cluster yielded two distinct subclusters.
The dominant subcluster exhibited a prevalent interaction pattern where visitors primarily created new infopanels before disengaging from the exhibit, as illustrated in~\cref{fig:case_1_large_model}.
In contrast, the smaller subcluster demonstrated similar prolonged engagement, characterized by frequent revisits to infopanels and changing of attributes.
The analytical procedure was repeated for a third iteration, reaching three levels of depth before concluding, as illustrated in~\cref{fig:case_1_clusters}.
% The previously established analytical procedures were repeated for another iteration, reaching three levels deep and, after merging, two clusters are revealed, before concluding, as illustrated in~\cref{fig:case_1_clusters}.
Two key observations emerged from the analysis.

\noindent 
\textbf{Key insight 1: Limited engagement with the exhibit}. 
The exploration revealed that the most frequent interaction pattern consisted of users opening infopanels without engaging in subsequent actions or further exploration, as illustrated in~\cref{fig:case_1_large_model}.
The SiN design experts concluded that the current version of the exhibit does not sufficiently capture or sustain user engagement beyond the initial interaction phase.
They hypothesized that SiN’s functionality may be too complex for visitors to comprehend without explicit instruction, potentially explaining the smaller clusters characterized by longer interaction sessions, presumably corresponding to guided use.
Similarly, the VLC experts noted that visitors appeared to struggle with sustained engagement, suggesting that the exhibit implicitly relies on a degree of self-directed or self-regulated learning competence, which may not be adequately supported by the current design.
Visitors were unable to independently formulate tasks or questions to maintain engagement with SiN.

To address this limitation, the SiN design experts proposed the integration of an interactive onboarding guide to better support initial user engagement.
The VLC experts further recommended the inclusion of structured guidance throughout the interaction experience.
This includes embedding cognitively engaging tasks, reflective questions, or exploratory prompts that can be addressed through direct interaction with the exhibit.
Prior work has demonstrated the value of such task-driven approaches and their resulting interaction patterns~\cite{Yu25Revealing}.

\noindent 
\textbf{Key insight 2: Disparity in attribute utilization}.
The second critical observation concerned the significant discrepancy in the usage of different attributes in the infopanel.
Specifically, the \textit{Gender} attribute was consistently substantially underutilized compared to the \textit{Year} attribute. 
The SiN design experts hypothesized two potential explanations for this behavior, both of which raise important concerns.
One explanation could be that visitors fail to recognize that the \textit{Gender} attribute, which is presented as small circular icons, is interactively adjustable.
Whereas the \textit{Year} attribute, presented as a traditional slider, is more immediately perceivable as adjustable. 
This hypothesis suggests a usability issue that can be directly addressed through interface design improvements.
A second explanation is that the lack of interaction with the \textit{Gender} attribute may indicate a lack of user interest in exploring gender-related data.
This hypothesis was particularly significant given that the central objective and original motivation of the exhibit was to support the exploration of gender differences and raise awareness about gender equality in Sweden. %, which served as the original motivation for its development. 
This indicates underlying structural design limitations in the exhibit that do not have trivial or straightforward solutions.

Additionally, the VLC experts noted that visitors expected SiN to support access to implicit data information, such as trends, based on previous conversations~\cite{Yu25Revealing}.
Visitors expressed a desire to derive insights extending beyond the static representations provided by traditional statistical tables and graphs.
Specifically, they sought to address more personal questions that require integrating multiple data points and calculating trends.
Therefore, the exhibit must support curiosity-driven inquiry, providing implicit information, addressing visitors' personal needs and motivations, and delivering structured learning experiences.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

While our VA system supports exploration of both structured and unstructured interaction logs and meets the analytical goals, design considerations and limitations are relevant to discuss.
This section highlights these and also considers potential future directions of research.

\noindent
\textbf{Expressiveness vs. effectiveness.} 
In this work, we utilize arc diagrams with fixed node ordering to represent process models, facilitating compact visual comparisons across clusters.
While this enhances readability and comparison by retaining consistency of representation across multiple process graphs, it reduces expressiveness~\cite{Card09Information} compared to formal representations, e.g., Petri nets.
These alternatives offer richer semantics, such as parallelism and branching logic, but often produce complex, less interpretable graphs~\cite{Miksch24Visual}.
Given our goal of enabling interactive model comparison, we prioritized concise, consistent visual encoding to support exploration without overwhelming the user.
% Future work could explore hybrid solutions that retain some formal semantics while maintaining visual simplicity by utilizing novel interaction techniques.

\noindent
\textbf{Generalizability of clustering techniques.} 
In this work, we explored agglomerative hierarchical clustering, leveraging its tree-like structure to support stepwise navigation, refinement, and user-guided merging of clusters.
However, partitional algorithms (e.g., k-means) operate via iterative assignment and centroid updates, producing flat clusterings without inherent hierarchical structure.
Consequently, they lack a linkage structure (e.g., dendrogram) necessary for supporting our visual navigation model.
On the other hand, unlike agglomerative methods, divisive approaches do not require full precomputation of the hierarchy, which may offer improved scalability for large datasets.
Top-down approaches, such as bisecting k-means~\cite{Savaresi04Comparative}, could be an alternative to experiment further.
Since the choice of clustering algorithm may lead to different groupings, future work could include a comparative evaluation with alternative strategies, such as divisive hierarchical clustering (e.g., bisecting k-means), to assess differences in structure quality, computational efficiency, and usability.
%Unlike agglomerative methods, divisive approaches do not require full precomputation of the hierarchy, which may offer improved scalability for large datasets.
Additionally, interaction improvement can further facilitate exploration and interpretation of cluster hierarchies, aiding users in understanding which process models fall under a given cluster.
One possibility would be to implement interactive features, such as hover-based brushing and linking, to reveal the process models associated with all descendant subclusters.

\noindent
\textbf{Generalizability to other domains}.
Our method has been developed and evaluated within the context of interaction logs.
While the underlying framework is domain-agnostic, one future study could be to evaluate its generalizability to other interaction datasets, such as online learning platforms, where exploration strategies may differ significantly.

\noindent
\textbf{Alternative validation techniques}.
We currently employ conformance checking to assess alignment between individual sequences and representative process models.
An alternative could be to use precision and fitness metrics with each model.
Fitness values can indicate how well the model reflects the observed behavior, and precision can quantify the extent to which the model generalizes beyond the log data.

\noindent
\textbf{Follow-up evaluation}.
In addition to the case studies, further empirical validation is needed to assess the impact of our findings.
A promising direction involves follow-up analysis after launching the interactive guide within the exhibit, comparing in-the-wild interaction patterns pre- and post-update.
% This would allow for a direct comparison of in-the-wild interaction patterns before and after the exhibit update.
A key dataset limitation is the ambiguity of session boundaries, as sessions may involve multiple users interacting consecutively or simultaneously, introducing noise into strategy extraction.
Future work could explore automated segmentation methods to disentangle such composite sessions and improve analytical accuracy.

\begin{comment}
\noindent
\textbf{Visual scalability}.
The current design focuses on a fixed number of high-level events, e.g., opening and revisiting of infopanels, and attribute changes.
As the number of attributes increases, visual scalability can become a concern. 
Enlarging the event alphabet may lead to visual clutter in both the arc diagrams and the sequence views, complicating interpretation and comparison.
However, we argue that a core set of event types is sufficient for characterizing exploration strategies in uncontrolled, in-the-wild interaction data. 
While incorporating additional event types may enable more fine-grained analysis and reveal further insights, our primary objective is to understand how individuals navigate and interact with the exhibit interface. 
We focus on behavioral patterns rather than the specific contextual parameters or content of the attributes being explored.
As such, the current level of abstraction strikes a deliberate balance between expressiveness and visual clarity.
Nevertheless, to mitigate visual clutter caused by such a scaling issue, future designs could experiment with the level of detail and adaptive visualization~\cite{Yanez25The} to facilitate exploration while maintaining interpretability.

\noindent
\textbf{Memory usage.} 
Scalability concerns also arise from memory constraints when dealing with large volumes of interaction log data. 
Loading all event sequences can be inefficient and hinder real-time system responsiveness and interactivity.
This can be addressed by displaying the sequences relevant to the currently explored cluster or subcluster.
Additionally, visual scalability challenges emerge when rendering dendrograms and heatmaps derived from large numbers of sequences.
To mitigate this, we introduce a set of visual abstractions, including partial dendrograms, partial heatmaps, and glyph-based representations.
However, the overview is lost in this summarization, and it inherently limits the user's ability to perceive the global structure.
This trade-off reflects a general challenge in scalable visualization design, where reducing complexity may hide details.
A balance between overview and detail, while maintaining user awareness of such limitations, is essential.
\end{comment}


\noindent
\textbf{Scalability}.
Potential scalability concerns in our system design arise both in terms of visual representation and memory usage when handling larger or more complex interaction data.
The current DFG representation uses four high-level event types to balance interpretability and expressiveness, effectively capturing user exploration strategies in uncontrolled settings.
A more detailed event taxonomy could support finer-grained analysis, but risks visual clutter and reduced clarity of representations.
This work focuses on behavioral patterns rather than specific contextual parameters or attribute values.
Additionally, system-level scalability is limited by memory and rendering constraints when visualizing large interaction datasets.
To maintain responsiveness, we show only sequences relevant to a selected cluster and employ visual abstractions, such as partial dendrograms, heatmaps, and glyphs, to reduce complexity. 
While this improves performance, it limits the global overview, highlighting a broader trade-off between scalability and interpretability. 
Future work may address this through adaptive or level-of-detail techniques~\cite{Yanez25The}.
Finally, DTW is used to measure sequence similarity for its robustness to varying sequence length~\cite{Chotirat05Three}, future work could investigate normalization to improve robustness~\cite{Kraprayoon24Improving}.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper has presented a VA approach that integrates hierarchical clustering and process mining to support the stepwise, interactive exploration of unstructured interaction log data. 
We employ a tailored DTW-based similarity measure and implement the proposed approach in the VA prototype system, reVISID.
The system facilitates (1) incremental exploration and navigation in the cluster hierarchy; (2) interpretation and comparison of clusters and process models; (3) investigation and connection between the cluster and individual sequences; and (4) incorporation of domain knowledge to identify potential exploration strategies.
The applicability and effectiveness of the proposed approach were demonstrated through two case studies involving the exhibit designer, developer, and domain experts.
